The primary constraint

Commercial and margin

Margin is the constraint, and it is the one that makes the others expensive. The business does not know what a job costs while it is running, so pricing is set on instinct and corrected after the fact. That produces months that look strong and months that look tight, with no clear reason for the difference.

What this usually costs is not visible on the P&L as a single line. It shows up as work that was never profitable being repeated, as discounting that becomes a habit rather than a decision, and as growth that adds revenue without adding profit. Businesses in this position often grow their way into a cash problem.

It ranks first because it sits upstream. Fixing delivery in a business that does not know which work is worth doing simply makes it more efficient at the wrong things.

This week

Three things to check

Specific to the primary constraint. Each one is answerable inside a week and none of them requires a project.

  1. Take the last ten completed jobs and work out what each one actually made. Include delivery hours at real cost, not at estimate. If more than two of the ten lost money, the pricing model is the problem rather than the sales pipeline.
  2. List every discount given in the last quarter and who approved it. If most were approved by the same person, or by nobody, discounting has become a habit rather than a decision. That is a control problem and it is fixable in a fortnight.
  3. Check when your prices were last reviewed against your costs. If it is more than twelve months, and your costs have moved, you already know the answer. Quantify the gap before deciding what to do about it.
What you told us

Set against what the scores say

You said the problem is

Delivery capacity. The team is stretched and we keep missing dates, so we need more people.

The scores say

Commercial and margin ranks first, delivery second. Adding capacity to unprofitable work increases the cost of the constraint rather than removing it.

This is the most common gap we see, and it is not a criticism. Capacity is what a stretched team feels, so it is what gets reported upwards. But the diagnostic suggests the business is busy with work that was priced before anyone knew what it cost to deliver. Hiring into that pattern makes the problem larger and harder to reverse.

What we would do

On these findings we would start with an Operational Clarity Audit focused on the commercial model: margin by client and service, the true cost to deliver, and where pricing has drifted from cost. That produces the baseline the rest of the work is measured against, and it will settle the capacity question with evidence rather than opinion.
